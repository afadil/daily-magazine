Antigravity 2.0 and the Flash that beat last year's Pro.

Google used I/O's headline slot to put numbers next to "small and fast." Gemini 3.5 Flash lands at 76.2% on Terminal-Bench 2.1, 83.6% on MCP Atlas, and 1,656 Elo on GDPval-AA — beating Gemini 3.1 Pro across nearly every coding and agent benchmark while running 4× faster than comparable frontier models, and up to 12× faster inside Antigravity.

If you've been running an orchestration loop yourself, this is the shape of the abstraction the rest of the market just settled on.

Pricing: $1.50 / $9 per 1M tokens, 1M context. The three-month dev calendar just got rewritten in real time.

Cisco SD-WAN authentication bypass — patch is non-optional.

CISA has added CVE-2026-20182 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Controller and Manager contain an authentication bypass with a perfect 10.0 CVSS — an unauthenticated remote attacker reaches administrator privileges, full stop. Weird Science · NASA / Webb
10

Soot in the wrong galaxy.

Astronomers used JWST to find polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons — PAHs, the carbon-rich soot of the universe — in Sextans A, the most chemically primitive dwarf galaxy ever surveyed for them. That's a problem for the textbook story. PAHs were supposed to require metals and time. Sextans A has neither.

The new images show the PAHs aren't smeared across the galaxy the way they are in metal-rich systems. They sit in dense, light-years- wide pockets — suggesting some of the chemistry that ends up in terrestrial planet formation can start running long before the standard astrophysical clock says it should. A second data point this week: a "platypus" sample of early-universe galaxies that resist classification at all.

Architecture in the Wild

Inside iMARS — the AI engineering stack Cloudflare runs on itself.

Cloudflare Engineering · May 2026 · 11-month rollout

Cloudflare published a deep technical postmortem-cum-prospectus this week on iMARS — the Internal MCP Agent/Server Rollout Squad — the tiger team they assembled to integrate AI across their engineering organization. Eleven months in, the numbers are the kind of thing you can actually compare yourself against.

The headline operational figure: 3,683 active internal users in the last 30 days. That is 60% of the entire company and 93% of R&D. The four-week rolling average of merge requests climbed from ~5,600 per week before the rollout to over 8,700 — a 55% lift in throughput without a corresponding lift in headcount.

The architectural piece worth lifting: their code review pipeline isn't a single mega-prompt. When a merge request opens, a coordinator agent fans out to up to seven specialized reviewers — security, performance, code quality, docs, release management, compliance, and the internal Engineering Codex — and de-duplicates their findings into one structured review comment. The coordinator also judges severity, so the human reviewer doesn't drown in nits.

Their cost-management discipline is the other lesson. Frontier models (Opus 4.6, GPT-5.4) handle security-sensitive and architecturally complex reviews. Kimi K2.5 on Workers AI handles roughly 15% of the reviewer traffic — mostly docs review — at a fraction of the frontier cost. They are running a real per-task model routing layer, not a single-vendor lock-in.

Why this matters beyond Cloudflare: the iMARS post is the most concrete public artifact yet on what "AI-native engineering org" actually looks like at staff+ scale. Most published writeups in this category have been vendor case studies. This is the customer publishing their own playbook, with cost numbers and named models. If your platform team is asked next quarter "what does a real internal AI rollout look like?" — this is now the citation.

"Rather than relying on one model with a massive, generic prompt, we launch up to seven specialised reviewers… managed by a coordinator agent that deduplicates their findings, judges severity, and posts a single structured review comment."
